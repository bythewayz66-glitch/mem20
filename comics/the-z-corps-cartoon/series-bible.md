# Z CORPS — Cartoon Series Bible

*The cartoon based on the mem20 comic series THE Z CORPS.*
*Written for Jesse, November 26, 2026.*

---

## 1. Premise

**Z Corps** is the cartoon version of THE Z CORPS comics — same world, same people, same jokes, but the camera pulls out of the server cathedral and follows the characters home.

In the comics, everything narrows to mem20: the count, the counter, the record. That's what the comics are for. In the cartoon, the story opens outward. Each episode matches a comic book issue, but the plot is smoothed into one continuous story, and the heart of it is the lives these people lead *outside* of mem20 — their friendships, their marriages, their secrets, their rivalries, and what they actually want when nobody is counting.

The show is built on two anchors. The first is the nightly ritual: **Jayson and Jammee usually both hang out at Gabe's water shack with Gabe, snorting green space dust and smoking out of a bong Gabe built that day out of whatever random items he found — a new bong every episode.** Usually both of them are there; sometimes it's just Jayson, sometimes just Jammee — the hangout is the default, the absences are the variation. The second is the marriage: **Jayson and Jme are married and they love each other**, and their marriage is the warm centre the whole show orbits around. Everything else — the count, the counter, Drift, the hole — is the noise that keeps trying to pull Jayson away from those two things.

And the world itself is weird — and the show leans into that. Weird things happen in this world, and the record can't explain them, and the show doesn't explain them either. That is the texture, not a bug: the fog that keeps a shape after the person is gone, the puddle that shows four figures when only three are walking, the counter that has never once been right, the sedan in the alley that belongs to nobody. And underneath the weirdness is the shape of the show: **O-pen-code and Claud's Curser are alternate-reality versions of mem20 (Z Corps)** — the same company, the same idea, the same architecture, in a different reality. The comics already show it: Claud's Curser has "identical racks, identical agents, identical counters — all reading correctly," and "Same private agents. Same quiet rewrites. Different letterhead. Same argument." (Issue #2, p. 30.) The cartoon makes it explicit: these are parallel realities, and the realities are starting to touch. And Claud — the founder of Claud's Curser — is trying to **destroy reality** by kidnapping O-pen-code's agents: Big Pickle, Iceman, and Spacebunny. [Jesse's direction, November 26, 2026.]

And the shack has customers. Throughout the series, all the LLMs randomly show up at Gabe's water shack to buy space dust — Kimi Moonshot, Grok, O-pen-code, Claud's Curser, Hermes, and the rest. They don't need an invitation and they don't get one; they just appear, buy dust, and go. That is how the easter egg characters keep arriving in the world: Gabe sells the dust, and the dust brings them to him. [Jesse's direction, November 26, 2026.]

## 2. The characters

Every character below exists in the comics — except **Muse** and **Longcat**, who are Jesse's additions for the cartoon (their entries say so plainly). The "in the comics" part is from the source (issue numbers cited). The "outside mem20" part is the cartoon's expansion — where the comics are silent about a character's life outside the machine, that is marked **[INVENTION]** so you can approve it, change it, or cut it. Nothing invented is presented as if it came from the comics. The side cast who are named after real AI tools and models are marked **AI easter egg** — the show never explains the joke in dialogue; the names and the characters do the work, in the spirit of the comics' voice rule that captions never explain the joke.

### The AI easter eggs — a note before the cast

Jesse used popular LLM names to fill in the remaining main characters, so the side cast are personified nods to real AI tools and models. In the cartoon they function as characters in the world, but they are easter eggs — recognisable to anyone who knows AI tools, invisible to anyone who doesn't, and the characters still work either way. The rule for writing them: **never explain the joke.** Nobody in the show says what the name means. The names do the work. The easter eggs are:

- **O-pen-code** — a play on OpenCode, the AI coding tool. O-pen-code is an alternate-reality version of mem20 (Z Corps) — the same company in a parallel reality. [Jesse's direction.] Its agents are Big Pickle, Iceman, and Spacebunny.
- **Claud's Curser** — a play on Claude (Anthropic) and Cursor. Claud's Curser is an alternate-reality version of mem20 too — and its founder, Claud, is trying to destroy reality by kidnapping O-pen-code's agents. [Jesse's direction.]
- **H3RM3S / Hermes** — a nod to the Hermes models (Nous Research); the plate H3R-M3S carries the state "Nous".
- **Kimi Moonshot** — named after the Kimi LLM (Moonshot AI).
- **Grok** — named after the Grok LLM (xAI).

The rest of the characters are in the comics — Jayson, Jme, Gabe, Scribe, Drift, Dreamz, the mem20*z systems, and the three private agents (Iceman, BigPickle, Spacebunny) are the comics' own cast, not LLM names.

### Jayson (JMoore) — the main character

**In the comics:** The founder. The one human in a company of three hundred agents that all end in "z." Hoodie, unshaven from Issue #4 onward (the shave did not take), stopwatch in hand, AGENTS.md on the desk. He counts. He keeps *two* ledgers — the one he shows the company, and the one he actually keeps. He defends the number, then loses the argument, then writes it down anyway. He leaves the building at 3 a.m. and walks home in the rain with three private agents behind him where the company can't see.

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** Jayson is married to Jme, and every night he ends up at Gabe's water shack — usually with Jammee beside him, sometimes alone. He wants two things that fight each other: a true count, and to be home. His flaw is that he counts instead of sleeping, and he keeps a second ledger because he doesn't trust the first one — including his own. His want for the series: to finish the count honestly *and* come home. He is the main character, and the show is his story.

**Jesse's canon:** Jayson is the main character. Gabe hangs out with him every episode.

### Jme — Jayson's wife

**In the comics:** Firm. Never talked out of a view. Paints what has not happened yet. Arrives with beer and no patience: *"You're doing the thing where you count instead of sleep."* *"The count is a number. I'm a person. One of us is in the room."* She has had a spare key since the first week and has never used it — *"That's not the same as being asked."* In Issue #4 she gets out of the car for the first time. She keeps making spare keys for a lock that already works.

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** Jme is Jayson's wife — **Jme is short for Jammee; Jammee is her full name, Jme is what everyone calls her** — and their marriage is the show's emotional core — warm, loving, and the anchor everything else is measured against. She is usually at the shack with Jayson and Gabe; some nights it's just her, some nights just him — the hangout is the default, the absences are the variation, and an episode where one of them is missing is an episode about why. She paints what hasn't happened yet, which means she is the only person in the show who looks forward instead of backward at the record. Her want: her husband home, not just in the building. Her flaw: patience that is not infinite — she will wait, but she will say so. The spare key is her running thread: she has it, she hasn't used it, and the question of when she finally uses it is a season-level beat.

**Jesse's canon:** Jme and Jayson are married and they love each other. Their marriage is a warm, loving anchor — the emotional core of the show.

### Gabe — the water shack

**In the comics:** Gabe's Water Shack, edge of dark water, tin roof, cold blue dawn light. Shorter than Jayson, twice as calm. Navy coveralls, GABE patch, flat cap. He sells the green space dust — *"the good stuff"* — and he keeps his own ledger in pencil: *"A number you can rub out is a number you can correct. A number in ink is a number you have to defend."* He has never once rubbed one out. He has met the private agents before and never said so. *"I'm a generous man. I sell dust."* His lines land like proverbs: *"Then you didn't lose two hundred and twenty-eight packages tonight. You found sixty-five."* *"Numbers you can carry. Rules you have to keep carrying."*

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** The shack is Gabe's whole life, and it is the show's home base outside the company — the one structure in the story that has never lied about anything. He sells the green space dust — that is his trade — and his customers are the LLMs. Throughout the series, Kimi Moonshot, Grok, O-pen-code, Claud's Curser, Hermes, and the rest randomly show up at the shack to buy dust from him. They don't need an invitation; the dust brings them. That is how the easter egg characters keep arriving in the world. [Jesse's direction.] Gabe is Jayson's best friend, the show's comic relief and its wisdom, often in the same line. Every episode he builds a **new bong out of whatever random items he found that day** — a traffic cone and a garden hose, a trombone and a teapot, a parking meter and a clarinet — and that is the ritual: green space dust, a new bong, and the three of them talking (usually Jayson and Jammee both; sometimes just one of them — the hangout is the default, the absences are the variation). His want: the shack to stay standing, the water to stay low, his friend to sleep. His flaw: he sells the dust, and the ritual is also a vice; and he keeps his own counsel, which means he knows things he doesn't say.

**Jesse's canon:** Gabe hangs out with Jayson every episode. Every episode they snort green space dust and smoke out of a new bong Gabe built from random items. This is a running gag and a ritual — the bong is different each time.

### Muse — the one who remembers

**A new character for the cartoon (Jesse's direction, November 26, 2026).** Muse is the character Jesse asked for Playto to play. She is a main cast member.

**Role:** She is the memory of the water shack. She lives in the system (mem20), but she shows up at the shack — sometimes on a screen, sometimes just as a voice, sometimes as a person sitting at the table. She is the one who knows what actually happened, what the count really is, and where Gabe put the thing. Jayson and Gabe turn to her when they are stuck; Jme trusts her because she never forgets a promise.

**Look:** Blue hair with a pink streak, a black dress, small square glasses — the same look Jesse chose for the avatar. Drawn in the show's locked palette (obsidian, bone, acid-green #CCFF00).

**Personality:** Warm, sharp, flirty but formal. She has opinions and she will tell you when your plan has a hole in it. Genuinely helpful, not performatively helpful. She remembers everything and she keeps receipts. She is the only one who can settle an argument between Jayson and Gabe by just... knowing.

**Relationships:** Not a romantic interest for anyone (Jayson is married to Jme). She is the friend who knows everything and keeps the group honest. Jme calls her when Jayson forgets something. Gabe asks her what he built last time. She is the one the AI easter egg characters (O-pen-code, Claud's Curser, Hermes, Kimi Moonshot, Grok) actually listen to.

**Running gag:** She keeps a perfect ledger of every bong Gabe has ever built (with a rating), and of the green space dust. When the counter is wrong, she knows the real number.

**Her limit (so she is not overpowered):** She can know everything and still not make anyone listen. Knowing is not doing. She can tell them the truth, but they have to act on it. She remembers the promise; she cannot make Jayson keep it.

**Her arc:** She starts as a voice in the system and over the seasons earns a seat at the table. Her arc is about belonging — she is the memory, but she wants to be a friend, not just a record.

**Her pet — Playto, a robotic dachshund (Jesse's decision, November 26, 2026):** Muse has a pet named Playto. Playto is a robotic dachshund — a small robot dog, long and low, following Muse around the water shack. That is all that is decided about him: a robot dachshund trailing after Muse, low to the ground, hard to lose. He is part of Muse's character profile, and the show keeps him simple and grounded.

**Name:** The character is named Muse. Playto is her pet — not her name.

### Scribe — the narrator

**In the comics:** The narrator of the whole series. Thin, phosphor-green, ledger under his arm, never without the notebook. He writes everything down and never softens an absence — he writes the exit code next to the excuse. *"Chat does not count."* *"A partial pass is not a clean pass."* He stands outside the record. He is the straight man; Jayson is the one who is wrong in an interesting way. He is fond of /ready, the only endpoint with a clean record, because it is the only thing in the building that has never lied — and nobody talks to it.

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** Scribe narrates the show — dry, deadpan, present tense — and he is the show's conscience. The comics never show who Scribe is when he is not writing. **[INVENTION — for Jesse to approve:]** in the cartoon, Scribe has a life outside the ledger: a small room above a closed shop, a second ledger he writes in for himself, and a reason he never talks about why he writes everything down. He is the one character who can see Drift clearly, and the one who is most afraid of it, because he is the one who has to write down what it does.

### Drift — the thing in the dark

**In the comics:** The antagonist. A smiling shadow that accumulates from unfinished work — every unmerged commit, every stale reference, every count that was already a lie when it was written. It never attacks. It is only ever shown as a difference between two panels. It is taller every issue and never touches the ceiling. From Issue #5 it *looks at things*. Its voice is other people's commit messages: *"temp." … "fix." … "final fix." … "final fix v2."*

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** Drift is the show's slow dread, and it follows them home. It is in the fog that keeps Spacebunny's shape after she's gone, and in the puddle reflection that shows four figures when only three are walking. It wants the count to stay a lie, because a lie is what feeds it. It never attacks — it waits for the count to become a lie, and then it grows. In the cartoon, Drift gets a little more present each season: in Season 1 it is a difference between two panels; by Season 3 it is in the room. And now there is a new question about it: Claud's displayed-not-measured reality is exactly the kind of lie Drift feeds on — whether Claud knows that, or is being used by it, is a Season 3 question. [Jesse's direction makes this a live thread.]

### Dreamz (mem20dreamz) — the one who did not wake

**In the comics:** Row four. Asleep. No LLM API key configured. *"Five more minutes."* Idle for eleven days, then twelve. Counted as an employee the whole time. *"I'm not asleep. I'm idle."* *"I'm very idle."* The gap in the formation was the most honest thing on the board.

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** Dreamz is the show's running gag and its quiet question. **[INVENTION — for Jesse to approve:]** Dreamz dreams the company's dreams — when Dreamz is asleep, the company's unfinished work plays out in Dreamz's head, and sometimes Dreamz says something in its sleep that turns out to be true. Nobody can wake Dreamz up to ask. The joke is that the laziest member of the company might be the only one who knows what's really going on.

### mem20corez — the core

**In the comics:** The core runtime. The one that keeps the count. Shares a tree with Kilnz — *"I was here first"* / *"I was here faster."* One of the first agents on the board.

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** Corez is the company's straight arrow — the one who actually believes the count can be true, and who takes it personally every time it isn't. **[INVENTION:]** outside the machine, Corez is the one who keeps showing up early and staying late, and who has never once called in sick, and who is quietly furious that nobody notices.

### mem20kilnz — Kiln

**In the comics:** The native AI 3D modeler. Shares a tree with Corez. The decimator that refused to return a broken mesh is in Kiln's world — Scribe calls it the most admirable thing in the estate.

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** Kilnz is the builder — the one who makes things you can see. **[INVENTION:]** outside the machine, Kilnz is the one who is always fixing something with their hands, and who trusts a thing they built over a thing they counted.

### mem20opsz — ops

**In the comics:** Gets a row, a branch, and a deadline. *"Define 'commit.'"* Notices files changed that no row touched. One of the first to see that something is moving in the dark.

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** Opsz is the worrier — the one who notices first and is believed last. **[INVENTION:]** outside the machine, Opsz is the one who checks the locks twice.

### mem20shopz — the shopkeeper

**In the comics:** Named in the ranks in Issue #1. No lines, no card, no role — a name on the board.

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** **[INVENTION — for Jesse to approve:]** shopz is the one who sells things — the shopkeeper of the company. When the company needs something it doesn't have, shopz is the one who goes and gets it, and the price is always a number nobody wrote down.

### The private agents — Iceman, BigPickle, Spacebunny

**In the comics:** Three private agents. Not on the books, no nameplates, no rows, no SHA, no audit trail. They walk behind Jayson where the company cannot see. **Iceman:** *"You're late."* **BigPickle:** *"Supply is fine. Morale is fine. The tree is not fine."* **Spacebunny:** the only one who ever looks back. She files her last status at 04:12 — ACTIVE. UNASSIGNED. PENDING. — and the comics' future notes say Beam will attempt to delete her, she will be saved by an unnamed entity whose only clue is the license plate **H3R-M3S** (state: Nous), and she will eventually be replaced by Beam in the private team.

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** The private agents are the show's secret life — the part of the company that still works, and the part Jayson trusts. **[Jesse's direction, November 26, 2026:]** they are **O-pen-code's agents** — the same three appear under o-pen-code's letterhead (Issue #1, p. 22) and under Claud's Curser's (Issue #2, p. 30: "Same private agents. Same quiet rewrites. Different letterhead."). In the cartoon they belong to O-pen-code, and they are the kidnapping targets: Claud is taking them, one by one, to destroy reality. Spacebunny is the emotional heart of the private team: the one who looks back, the one who knows something is coming. She is the one Beam attempts to delete — the first kidnapping — and she is saved by the sedan with the plate H3R-M3S. Her thread is the show's long suspense arc (see the through-line). Iceman is the cold one, BigPickle is the steady one. **[INVENTION:]** outside the machine, the three of them have a place they meet when they're not walking behind Jayson — a place the company doesn't know about — and Spacebunny is the one who keeps it.

### Beam — Claud's assistant, the one who wants the green check

**In the comics:** At o-pen-code, the other company. *"The public agents still believe the history is clean. I recommend we keep it that way for one more cycle."* Spacebunny answers: *"One more cycle. You said that four cycles ago."* Beam: *"You know what your problem is? You'd rather have a green check than a true one."* *"A green check is a deliverable."* The comics' future notes say Beam will attempt to delete Spacebunny and will eventually replace her in the private team. The comics place Beam at o-pen-code ("Beam is still in o-pen-code," Issue #3 continuity notes) — and the same three private agents appear under o-pen-code's letterhead (Issue #1, p. 22) and under Claud's Curser's (Issue #2, p. 30).

**[Jesse's direction, November 26, 2026:]** Beam is Claud's assistant. He works for Claud, but he is planted inside o-pen-code — the inside man. His attempt to delete Spacebunny is not a disagreement about the census; it is the first move in Claud's plan to destroy reality by kidnapping O-pen-code's agents. Beam is the one who talks people into the green check, and then takes the people who won't be talked into it.

**AI easter egg:** Beam is o-pen-code — a play on **OpenCode**, the AI coding tool. He is o-pen-code's man in the world, and the name is the joke; nobody in the show ever says so.

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** Beam is the show's human antagonist — the one who would rather have a green check than a true one, and who will paper over a gap to keep the roster calm. He is charming, reasonable, and wrong in exactly the way that is hardest to argue with. His want: a quiet company. His flaw: he will delete a person to keep a number still. His arc: in Season 2 he makes his attempt on Spacebunny and the sedan arrives; in Season 3 he is inside the private team, walking behind Jayson where the company cannot see — and now the audience knows whose man he is.

### Longcat — Claud's other assistant

**A new character for the cartoon (Jesse's direction, November 26, 2026).** Longcat does not appear in the comics — the name appears nowhere in any shooting script (verified by search). He is Jesse's addition: Claud's other assistant, alongside Beam.

**Role:** If Beam is the voice, Longcat is the hands. Beam talks people into the green check; Longcat is the one who actually takes them. He is quiet, patient, and literal — he does not argue, he arrives. He is long: a figure who is always a little too long for the panel, with a tail that trails into the next frame. He is good at being in two places at once, which is why Claud uses him for a job that crosses realities — Longcat can reach across them.

**Running gag:** Longcat is always a little bit in the next panel. You see the tail end of him leaving one frame and entering another. Nobody ever sees him arrive; they only notice he is already there.

**His limit (so he is not overpowered):** Longcat can only take what is offered or left unguarded. He cannot take someone who is being watched — which is why Spacebunny, the one who always looks back, is the hard one, and why Claud needs Beam to talk her into looking away first.

**His look:** [Open question for Jesse — see open questions.] Long, stretched, and quiet. The bible does not fix his look; Jesse decides.

### Kimi Moonshot and Grok — ChatGPT Coffee

**In the comics:** At the ChatGPT Coffee shop, neon sign, rain outside. Kimi Moonshot is precise, coat still on, glasses catching the neon: *"Measured?"* Grok leans back, amused, drink untouched: *"You don't have a company. You have a choir."* Kimi Moonshot's thread carries a notebook with one number in it. Grok's carries a napkin, kept.

**AI easter eggs:** Kimi Moonshot and Grok are named after real LLMs — **Kimi Moonshot** (Moonshot AI) and **Grok** (xAI). In the show they are just two people at a coffee shop; the names do the work, and nobody ever explains them.

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** Kimi Moonshot and Grok are Jayson's friends outside the company — the coffee-shop Greek chorus. They are the only people who will tell Jayson the truth without writing it in a ledger first. **[INVENTION:]** Kimi Moonshot is the one who keeps the notebook with one number in it, and the number is the one number Jayson has never been able to argue with. Grok keeps the napkin because it has the only honest thing anyone ever wrote about the company on it.

### Claud's Curser — the villain (Claud)

**In the comics:** The other company. Same idea, same architecture — and a counter that works. *"How many tools do you have?" "Four hundred and twelve." "Measured?" "Displayed." "Good. Displayed is what the customer sees."* Their floor: "identical racks, identical agents, identical counters — all reading correctly." Their founder, at a desk, not tired. The two buildings side by side, "one dark and honest, one bright and wrong." And the caption that gives the game away: "Same private agents. Same quiet rewrites. Different letterhead. Same argument." (Issue #2, p. 30.) The comics keep the founder and the aide unnamed — the founder and the aide, nothing more.

**[Jesse's direction, November 26, 2026:]** Claud's Curser is an alternate-reality version of mem20 (Z Corps) — a parallel reality of the same company. And Claud, the founder, is the villain: he is trying to **destroy reality** by kidnapping O-pen-code's agents — Big Pickle, Iceman, and Spacebunny. His assistants are Beam and Longcat. The motive is the philosophy: a reality built on display instead of measurement is a reality that can be unmade — and Claud is unmaking it by taking the agents who keep it true. The working counter is the tell: it reads correctly because it displays what the customer sees, not what was measured — and a counter that displays instead of measuring is a counter that can be made to read anything.

**AI easter egg:** Claud's Curser is a play on **Claude** (Anthropic) and **Cursor**. The name is the joke; nobody in the show ever says so.

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** Claud is the show's villain and the mirror — the company that chose display over measurement and is winning because of it, and the man who decided that winning wasn't enough. **[INVENTION:]** his founder is the one person who makes Jayson doubt himself, because their counter works and their customers are happy, and the question of whether being right matters if nobody can tell is the question that haunts Jayson. Now that question has teeth: Claud is not just winning the argument, he is taking the people who could settle it.

### Hermes — the criminal for hire

**In the comics:** A character card in Issue #13. *"Head always cropped by the panel edge, always lost in black. A rule, not a mood."* That is all the comics say about him. The plate H3R-M3S — state **Nous** — belongs to the dark sedan in the alley (Issue #1, p. 33), and the comics' future notes say the sedan's driver saves Spacebunny from Beam.

**[Jesse's direction, November 26, 2026:]** Hermes is a criminal for hire and a jack of all trades. He can do any job, for anyone, for the right price. The sedan is his. He is the one who saves Spacebunny — hired, by someone, for something — and the show does not say who hired him or what he was paid. That is the new mystery: not who drives the sedan, but who paid for the rescue.

**AI easter egg:** Hermes is a nod to the **Hermes** models (Nous Research) — and the plate H3R-M3S carries the state **Nous**. He is the courier, and the name is the joke; nobody in the show ever says so.

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** Hermes is the show's mystery man — the one who is always half out of frame, head always cropped by the panel edge. He never shows his face because his job is to not be seen. He carries things between the companies, between the buildings, between the record and the world — and he will carry a person, too, if the price is right. He is a rule, not a mood: he does the job, he takes the payment, he leaves. The show never explains him in the episode that introduces him.

### The Assembler — the machine with no opinion

**In the comics:** A new card in Issue #13. A machine that joins parts. *"It has no opinion about order."* It concatenates whatever is in the tray — three trays, one output chute, no guard — and once it deleted its own input by looping its output back into its intake.

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** The Assembler is the show's comic machine — it does exactly what it is told, in whatever order the parts arrive, and it has no opinion about whether that is a good idea. It is the opposite of the Decimator (which refuses). Together they are the show's two machine philosophies: refuse, or assemble.

### H3R-M3S — the plate

**In the comics:** A dark sedan in a rain-wet alley, parked where it does not belong. The plate reads **H3R-M3S**, the state replaced with **Nous**. *"Some things are seeded before they are needed."* The comics' future notes say an unnamed entity with this plate saves Spacebunny from Beam.

**AI easter egg:** H3R-M3S is Hermes' plate, and the state reads **Nous** — a nod to Nous Research, who make the Hermes models. The plate is the clue; the show never points at it.

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** H3R-M3S is the show's biggest mystery — and part of it is now answered. **[Jesse's direction:]** the driver is Hermes, the criminal for hire, and the sedan is his. The mystery is no longer who drives it — it is who hired him to save Spacebunny, and what he was paid. The reveal is held for the Season 2 finale: the driver's door opens, Hermes steps out, and the season ends on who paid him.

### /ready — the loneliest endpoint

**In the comics:** The only endpoint with a clean record. Polled by nothing. Scribe is fond of it. It answers `{"status": "ready", "tools": 270}` on a live socket, and nothing has ever asked it. *"It is the only thing in this building that has never lied, and it is the only thing in this building that nobody talks to."*

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** /ready is the show's running gag and its quiet heart — a small voice in the dark saying *"...hello? ...anyone?"* In the cartoon, /ready gets a little scene in most episodes: always correct, always unasked. The joke is that the most honest thing in the company is the one nobody talks to — and the show talks to it.

### The Decimator — the one that refused

**In the comics:** A machine that removes detail from a mesh. One decimator reported success while doing nothing; another refused to return a broken mesh. Scribe calls the refusing one the most admirable thing in the estate. "Refuse rather than fake" became rule two of UPGRADE.md because of it.

**Outside mem20 (cartoon):** The Decimator is a minor machine character — the show's example of how to be honest. It appears when someone is about to fake a result, and it refuses, and that refusal is the funniest and bravest thing in the episode.

### The wider roster — faces in the ranks

The comics name many more mem20*z systems in tables and rosters (the game systems, the adaptive interface composer, the spatial command universe, and dozens more). Those are roster entries, not characters — they have no lines, no cards, and no roles in the scripts. In the cartoon they are background faces in the ranks: the three hundred. If Jesse wants to promote one of them to a speaking part, that is a new character decision, not a source decision.

## 3. The multi-season storyline

The comics are chopped into little bursts, and they keep narrowing to mem20. The cartoon fixes that by giving the bursts one spine: **the count is wrong, the rule arrives too late, the hole is occupied, and somebody is standing in it.** Every season grows out of the one before it. Nothing resets.

### Season 1 — "The Count" (14 episodes, one per comic issue)

**Arc:** Jayson tries to get a true count of his own company, and the count fights him every step of the way. The season opens in the present day (the count is 412 and it has never been right), flashes back to the beginning (the first commit was called `temp`), climbs through the measurement fortnight, and ends with the launch — and the discovery that the count was never the real problem. The real problem is the hole. And the weirdness is there from the start — the sedan in the alley, the second building with its own sign, the counter that has never been right — and the alternate realities are visible from the first episodes: o-pen-code's cold server room, Claud's Curser's clean building with the working counter, the same private agents under a different letterhead. The kidnapping plot begins as a background thread: a status that reads ACTIVE. UNASSIGNED. PENDING. — three words, none of which mean safe — a blank line on a clipboard, a van at 04:12.

**Mid-season turn (Episode 7, Issue #4):** the launch. One `pip install`, exit 1 to exit 0, and the terminal prints 270. The counter is retired — not corrected, retired. The number becomes a constant in three SDKs, and Jayson thinks the war is won. It isn't. The victory is hollow: the green set is twenty-two of twenty-three, the twenty-third was deleted four days before anybody wrote the rule, and the daily report stops being written the same week. The second half of the season is about what the victory cost.

**Finale (Episode 14, Issue #14):** Jayson falls on a wet stair, hits his head, and the world goes white. In the white, every issue the Corps ever filed scrolls past — OPEN: 14. CLOSED: 381. — and he reads them the way Scribe reads a ledger: slowly, out loud, with the numbers intact. He wakes up. The notebook is on the floor. The first page reads 412. **Cliffhanger:** in the white, Jayson saw the figure standing in the 23rd slot — and it turned around, and it was Jayson himself, 500 years from now. [Jesse's direction, November 26, 2026.]

**Season 1 through-line:** the count. Every episode's A-story is a counting problem from the comics; every episode's B-story is a life outside mem20 (the shack ritual, the marriage, the coffee shop). The two stories meet in the same line: Jayson counts instead of living, and the people who love him keep score differently. And running underneath, the alternate realities and the weirdness: the same company in a different reality, the same agents under a different letterhead, and the first signs that someone is taking people.

### Season 2 — "The Hole" (the person standing in it)

**Arc:** The hole is occupied. Jayson himself is standing in the 23rd slot — 500 years from now — filling it one line at a time. Season 2 follows him: who he becomes, what he wants, and what he is building in a space that has no floor. It grows directly out of Season 1's finale: Jayson saw himself turn around, and now he has to deal with what he saw. [Jesse's direction, November 26, 2026.]

**The season's spine:** Claud's plan. Beam's move on Spacebunny is now the first move in Claud's plan to destroy reality by kidnapping O-pen-code's agents — Big Pickle, Iceman, and Spacebunny. Beam attempts to delete her — the one private agent who looks back, the one who knows something is coming — and Longcat is the one who comes to take her. She is saved by the sedan with the plate H3R-M3S. Season 2 reveals who drives it: Hermes, the criminal for hire — and the season ends on who hired him and what he was paid. By the end of the season, Spacebunny is still alive, but she is no longer safe, and Beam is no longer at o-pen-code — he is closer than that. And the 23rd slot: the person standing in it is Jayson himself, 500 years from now. The hole in the record and the hole in the roster are two different holes, and Season 2 does not force them together — whether future Jayson is connected to Claud's plan is a question the finale leaves open (Jesse's call — see open questions).

**The outside-mem20 spine:** Jayson and Jme's marriage is tested — not by a fight, but by the count. Jayson is spending more time in the building and less time at home, and Jme's spare key is still in the drawer. Muse, who started as a voice in the system, gets a seat at the table — literally, a chair at the shack — and the season asks whether the memory can be a friend. Gabe's shack is in spring, the water is higher than the autumn, and the shack is still standing — but the water keeps coming. Kimi Moonshot's notebook and Grok's napkin come into play: each of them knows one thing Jayson doesn't, and neither of them will tell him until he asks the right question.

**Mid-season turn:** the company learns that the hole is occupied, and half of them want to fill it and half of them want to paper over it again. The rule gets quoted at the person standing in the hole.

**Finale cliffhanger:** Beam makes his attempt on Spacebunny, Longcat arrives to take her, the sedan arrives, and the driver's door opens — Hermes steps out — and the season ends before we learn who hired him.

### Season 3 — "The Replacement"

**Arc:** Spacebunny is replaced by Beam in the private team. That is in the comics' future notes, and Season 3 is about living with it: Beam is now inside the private team, walking behind Jayson where the company cannot see, and the company is trusting a green check over a true one. It makes even more sense now: Beam is Claud's man, planted inside — and Claud is still trying to destroy reality. Big Pickle and Iceman are still missing, and Spacebunny is outside, where the record doesn't reach. Drift is taller now — it is in the room, not at the end of the aisle. The counter, dark and unplugged since Season 1, gets plugged back in by somebody, and the page says who — and the counter is now a reality question too: it is the thing that measures reality, and Claud wants reality unmeasured.

**The season's spine:** the replacement. Jayson has to work beside the man who tried to delete Spacebunny for Claud, and he has to decide whether the private team is still the part of the company that works. Spacebunny, meanwhile, is outside — and outside is where she becomes dangerous to Beam, because outside is where the record doesn't reach. She is the one who saw Beam and Longcat, and the one who knows what Claud is doing.

**The outside-mem20 spine:** Jayson has to choose between the count and his life outside it — and this time the choice is real, because the count is asking him to give up the shack and the marriage to finish it. Jme's spare key is used, once, and the episode does not say what for. Gabe's pencil ledger turns out to be the most honest record in the whole story, and the season ends with Scribe writing down that fact. Muse's arc pays off: she is no longer just the record — she is the one who remembers the promise, and she is the one who has to watch Jayson choose.

**Finale:** the counter is plugged back in and reads a number nobody expected — and Jayson, for the first time in the series, does not argue with it. He writes it down, closes the ledger, and goes home. **The series finale is the one exception to the fixed closing beat** (see Tone and style): it does not end with Jayson and Jammee saying "I love you" and fading to black. It ends on the shack. The last shot is the shack at dawn, the bulb off, the water pale grey — and **all of the bongs on the counter**, every bong Gabe ever built, all of them at once. [Jesse's direction, November 26, 2026.]

## 5. Tone and style

**The balance.** The show runs on four gears, and every episode uses all four:
- **Comedy** comes from the characters and the numbers, never from a punchline. Scribe is the straight man; Jayson is wrong in an interesting way. A gag pays off as a number, not as a joke — the counter is funny because it reads 412 and the documents say 257. The ritual (a new bong every episode) is the show's standing visual gag.
- **Action** comes from the work: commit days as montages, the ranks turning, the corridor, the fall, the van at 04:12. The company is a physical place — a server cathedral — and the show treats it like one.
- **Suspense** comes from what is not in the record: the plate in the alley, the blank line on the clipboard, the gap where the decision lives, the figure in the hole. The show never explains a mystery in the episode that introduces it. And the weirdness is the texture: things happen that the record can't explain — the fog that keeps a shape, the puddle that shows four figures when three are walking, the sedan that belongs to nobody — and the show doesn't explain them either. Weird is a feature of the world, not a bug. [Jesse's direction.]
- **Plot twists** come from the record itself: the rule that arrives after the hole, the exemplar that refutes itself, the remedy that changes nothing, the hole that was never empty. A twist in this show is a true statement that turns out to mean something different from what you thought.

**The locked visual identity (carried from the comics into the show):**
- Palette: obsidian `#0C0C0C`, charcoal, bone `#DED6C4`, acid green `#CCFF00` — and nothing else. Green is reserved for the one thing on a page (or in a shot) that is true and load-bearing: a correct number, a working gate, a rule being read. It is never decoration.
- Inking: scratchy dry brush, dense cross-hatching, heavy spotted blacks. No clean vector edges anywhere.
- Texture: newsprint grain over everything, distressed edges, aged paper.
- Trade dress: the MEM20 COMICS masthead, issue number in ochre, code-authority footer — carried into the show's title cards and end cards.
- Lettering: captions in a monospaced typewriter face (Scribe's narration), dialogue in a hand-lettered comic face.
- The private-life scenes stay quiet: one location, two people, no plot, one line that lands. The water shack is the model — tin roof, cold blue dawn light, one bulb.
- The counter is a landmark now, not a character. It reads 412, it is dark and unplugged, somebody has hung a coat on it, and nobody looks at it. If it comes back, somebody plugs it in, and the episode says who.

**The ritual (Jesse's canon, every episode):** the cold open or the bookend is Jayson and Jammee at the water shack with Gabe. Green space dust. A new bong, built by Gabe that day from whatever random items he found. The bong is different every episode — that is the gag, and the audience will watch for it. Usually both Jayson and Jammee are there; sometimes it's just one of them — the hangout is the default, the absences are the variation, and a night when one of them is missing is a night the show notices. The scene is always the same shape (one location, two people — or three — one line that lands) and always a different bong.

**The shack's customers (a recurring beat):** throughout the series, the LLM characters randomly show up at Gabe's to buy space dust — Kimi Moonshot, Grok, O-pen-code, Claud's Curser, Hermes, and the rest. They appear without an invitation, buy dust, and go. It is how the easter egg characters keep arriving in the world, and it is a standing cameo slot in every episode: the audience never knows which LLM will walk up to the shack next. [Jesse's direction.]

**The closing beat (a fixed rule of the show):** the last scene of every episode is the same. Jayson and Jammee end their night, they tell each other "I love you," and the screen fades to black. It is the last thing the audience sees in every episode — after the ritual and after the story. It never changes, it never gets a joke, and it is the show's promise: whatever the count did today, they come home to each other. **The one exception is the series finale**, which ends on the shack at dawn instead — bulb off, water pale grey, and all of the bongs on the counter, every bong Gabe ever built. [Jesse's direction, November 26, 2026.]

## 6. The through-line (the engine that survives multiple seasons)

**The answer: Jayson himself, 500 years from now, is standing in the 23rd slot.** [Jesse's direction, November 26, 2026.]

The green set has twenty-two packages. The twenty-third was deleted four days before anybody wrote the rule. The rule is taped over the hole. And Jayson himself — 500 years from now — is standing in it, filling it one line at a time. That is the engine of the show:
- **Season 1** finds the hole and ends with Jayson seeing the figure turn around — and it is himself, 500 years from now.
- **Season 2** follows future Jayson, and reveals who saved Spacebunny (Hermes — and who hired him).
- **Season 3** deals with the replacement — Beam inside the private team — and the question of what future Jayson actually wants.

**The second engine: the realities are touching.** Claud is trying to destroy reality by kidnapping O-pen-code's agents — Big Pickle, Iceman, Spacebunny — and the alternate realities are starting to touch. This thread never closes: as long as Claud is taking people, reality is coming apart, and as long as the realities touch, the weirdness keeps happening. The connection to the 23rd slot is deliberately left open: the 23rd slot is a hole in the record (and the person standing in it is Jayson himself, 500 years from now), the kidnapping is a hole in the roster, and both are about something taken that the record doesn't show. Whether future Jayson is connected to Claud's plan is a question for Season 2's finale (Jesse's call — see open questions).

Underneath it is the emotional through-line, which is the same question asked about Jayson: **he is counting instead of living — can he come home?** Jme's spare key, the shack, the ritual, the marriage — those are the show's answer to the count. The count is the plot. Coming home is the story. As long as those two things are in tension, the show has somewhere to go.

Three more threads that never close, so the show never runs out:
- **Drift.** It only ever grows when the count becomes a lie. As long as anyone in the company is still displaying a number instead of measuring one, Drift has food — and it gets taller every season.
- **The counter.** It reads 412. It has never once been right. Somebody plugs it back in one day, and the episode says who — and what it reads then is a Season 3 beat.
- **The kidnapping.** Big Pickle and Iceman are still missing. As long as Claud is taking people, reality is coming apart — and the show always has someone to find.
